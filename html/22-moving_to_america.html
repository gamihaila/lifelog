Moving to America

As I neared the end of my PhD at the University of Toronto, I started thinking seriously about what would come next. The path into the job market for someone finishing a doctorate in database research at the time ran through a single mailing list. As I was scheduling my thesis defense, I heard from other students in our research group about dbGrads, part of a SIGMOD-run system called dbWorld that connected graduating database researchers with academic and industry opportunities. I registered without thinking much of it. Then one day, out of nowhere, I got an email from a hiring manager at the IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center in Hawthorne, New York, inviting me for an interview.

I went, gave a talk on my dissertation work, and met with a handful of research staff members and research engineers, all reporting to the same manager. One of the people I met with was Stu Feldman, who turned out to be Vice President for Computer Science across all of IBM Research, though I only half-grasped at the time what that meant. He was also, as I’d later learn, one of the people who had helped build Unix itself at Bell Labs, had written the first Fortran 77 compiler, and had created make. None of that came up in the room. He was remarkably relaxed about the whole thing. “I’m not going to make you jump through any more hoops,” he told me. “The other interviewers already did that. I’m just here to tell you what the expectations are like here: we understand that you will have your strong years and your weaker years, and you will not be penalized for that as long as you keep doing good work.”

During my internship at the Systems Research Center in Palo Alto in 1999, I had worked with a researcher named Krishna Bharat on something we called the Hilltop algorithm, a way of identifying authoritative pages on the web for the purposes of ranking search results. I remember seeing a cloth banner with Google’s logo hanging from one of the buildings on University Avenue that summer, back when the name still meant nothing to most people. A group of Google people invited Krishna out for lunch while I was still his intern, and not long after, he announced his resignation from SRC. He timed his departure deliberately, staying just long enough for us to finish the work and for me to give my final internship talk. He even helped me polish the PowerPoint presentation. His last day at SRC was the same day as mine.

Krishna joined Google that fall, when the company was still tiny. By the spring of 2000 I already had my offer in hand from IBM Research, the job I had actually wanted all along, and was finishing up my PhD. Then, in the early summer, Krishna invited me out for an interview at Google for a Research Scientist position. I flew out and presented my actual PhD thesis work, on publishing, locating, and querying networked information sources, rather than Hilltop, which would have interested them far more. I didn’t know it at the time, but Google was already in the process of acquiring the Hilltop patent from HP, the parent company of SRC.

After the interview I flew back to Romania for a visit. While I was there, an email arrived from Google asking me to come back for another round, a full day of back-to-back coding and systems design interviews. They paid for the trip. I remember one interviewer in particular, Urs Hölzle (who would go on to become Google’s eighth employee of record and eventually Senior Vice President of Technical Infrastructure, one of the most senior engineers in the company’s history), who asked me to choose between working on Search or working on Ads. When it was all over, I received an offer, for a Software Engineer position, a role I hadn’t even realized I was being considered for. I had been interviewing the whole time for Research Scientist.

With the IBM offer already in hand, I could compare them directly. Google’s cash offer was lower than IBM’s, and the Bay Area cost of living was considerably higher than Westchester County. To compensate, the offer from Google came with twenty thousand stock options, priced at eighty-five cents a share. At the time, those options meant nothing to me. There was no IPO on the horizon, not for years, and I had just spent years earning a doctorate. So I turned it down, choosing IBM Research instead. I’ll let you do the math what those stock options would have been worth when Google finally went public.

Soon after, I finished my PhD. My scholarship money ran out the moment I graduated, and so did something else: my apartment. I had been living in Graduate Family Housing on Charles Street with my fiancee, Grațiela, and graduation meant we had to be out by the end of September. We put our furniture into storage and went looking for somewhere to live while my US work visa, the one for IBM, was still being processed with no end in sight. After a lot of searching we found a furnished studio on Isabella Street for $1,500 a month, nearly triple what we had been paying as students. We had no income to speak of, only Grațiela’s PhD stipend, but we had no real choice, so we took it and started dipping into my credit cards to cover the difference.

The visa process was no faster then than it is now. There was exactly one path that could get someone across the border quickly: a NAFTA visa, available to citizens of Canada working in a defined list of professions. Software engineer was on that list. Computer science researcher was not. My job at IBM had been classified as research, which meant the fast path wasn’t available to me, no matter how qualified I was. It reminded me, in a way, of the Canadian immigration officer years before who had rejected my application the moment I called myself a computer scientist, and approved it the moment I said I could also work as a software engineer.

With money running out and no end in sight, I started looking for backup options, specifically jobs classified as software engineering rather than research, since those could get me into the country immediately. I wrote to all the people I knew in the States, and to my surprise, they all came through. You see, we were at the height of the dot-com boom, and everybody was a founder: Anthony Tomasic had started a company to build a system for automatically generating grocery orders from cooking recipes and wanted to connect that with Martha Steward’s TV show, Alon Halevy just founded Nimble Technologies to develop data integration solutions, and Dana was working for Adam Bosworth, who had left Microsoft with his team to found Crossgain. I wasn’t sold on Anthony’s business idea, but I had a lot of respect for Alon and I trusted Dana’s intuition. Alon, who was the external examiner for my PhD thesis invited me right away for an interview at Nimble. Dana told her boss about me and he called me from cell phone from his morning run the next day: he explained to me his vision for building Web applications from simple, generic components that can be deployed on different servers and would talk to each other using an XML-based protocol. I was intrigued by this idea, remember, this was in early 2000, before anybody heard of Roy Fielding’s thesis that formalized REST.

It so happened that both start-ups were in Seattle so they could share the travel expenses for my interviews. So I got on a plane and flew to Seattle, all expenses paid. First, I interviewed at Crossgain: they had me meet with several engineers and code on the whiteboard, then Adam invited me in his office to offer me a software engineer job. I told him I already have an offer for a research position from IBM but I took his written offer and told him I’ll give him an answer soon.

After the Crossgain interview I still had a day before the Nimble interview and I decided to drive to Vancouver for the day. Once there I took the ferry to Victoria Island. It was a great trip, but I got back to Seattle after midnight. I was wiped the next day at the interview, which may explain why I didn’t get any offer from Nimble.

I told my hiring manager at IBM Research what was happening: that I had an offer in hand elsewhere, that I intended to take it on a temporary basis just to get into the country, and that I planned to come back to IBM once my actual visa was sorted out.

That got their attention. Within a day or two, IBM had made a few calls to their immigration lawyers and come back with a solution: they would issue me a new offer letter, identical in salary and responsibilities to the one I already had, but with one change. My title would read “Software Engineer” instead of “Researcher.” On paper, nothing about my actual work would change. On paper was exactly what mattered. I drove to the border with the new letter in December, and the visa was issued on the spot. I worked as a “software engineer” for the first few months, doing exactly the research I had been hired to do, until the paperwork caught up and they quietly switched my title back. It was absurd, and it was also exactly the kind of absurdity that, after Romania, didn’t strike me as particularly strange.

Once the visa finally came through, Grațiela and I got married at City Hall. I drove down to White Plains alone to start the job. She followed by train a few weeks later, and nearly didn’t make it across the border: when the customs officer asked the purpose of her visit and she said she was going to see her husband, the officer told her flatly that visiting a husband was not what a tourist visa was for, that she needed a proper dependent visa instead. Somehow, she took pity on her, and let her through on the promise that she would return to Toronto after the holidays and apply for the right paperwork.

I was waiting for her at Pennsylvania Station, in the middle of the crowd, and we missed each other completely. I ended up having her name called out over the public address system before we finally found one another. I brought her home to our new apartment, a two-bedroom on Lake Street in White Plains.

I have to say, after living in Toronto for seven years, I found White Plains quite dull. There really wasn’t much to do, especially in the cold winter, other than hanging out in the Westchester Mall, the only place we could walk around and not freeze. Seeing the prices in the stores there got me thinking my large looking IBM salary wasn’t really all that much. Since we were just hastily married in City Hall before leaving Toronto, we started planning a wedding ceremony in church, and we were in the market for a wedding dress. We started looking at bridal catalogs and everything was in the thousands of dollars. The only affordable items were something called “HP”, which were only a few hundreds. “Let’s get one of these”, Grațiela was saying, happy that she found something we could afford. When we looked more carefully we figured out what “HP” meant: “Head Piece”. So not a dress. Oh, well, in the end we did find a very nice cream-white dress at Macy’s, not technically a wedding gown, but it did the job.

After the New Year’s day, 2001, Grațiela was due back to Toronto, for her doctoral research and also to get a proper dependent visa. She only stayed a couple weeks there and came back, after arranging with her thesis advisor that she will continue working remotely and meet once per quarter. So a few months passed uneventfully until one day Grațiela started feeling sick and we went to see a doctor at the local clinic. After he consulted her, he came looking for me in the waiting room:

“Mr. Mihaila?”, he called.

“Yes”, I said, getting up, not knowing what to expect.

“Well, sir, I consulted your wife, and everything is fine, no need to worry.”

“Oh, so what was it?”, I asked, relieved.

“She’s expecting! You’re going to have a baby, she’s due around November 1st.”

“Wow! Thank you!”, I blurted, taken by surprise.

“Congratulations to both of you!”, said the doctor, and left.

Grațiela came out right after, in a daze. It took us a bit of time to process the news: we were extremely happy, of course, even though we didn’t “plan for it”. What that meant, though, was that for our church wedding service had to scheduled sooner than we were initially thinking, and her cream-white dress from Macy’s was going to be a little tight. No matter, though.

We had a lovely wedding service, with Laura and her husband Serghi as god-parents. Yes, the same Laura that introduced us, back in Toronto. They flew to New York for a few days, and we had a very small party with a couple of other Romanian friends from New York City. Our parents could not attend, sadly, there was no time for them to get tourist visas.

We started going to the regular checkups and eventually to a sonogram where the radiologist showed us our baby’s feet on the computer screen, singing “These feet are made for walking, and that’s just what they’ll do, one of these days these feet are gonna walk right over you!”. Talk about foreshadowing!

Everything was going smoothly, my new job at IBM Research was interesting, feeling very much like the work I was doing in graduate school, not necessarily content-wise, more like effort-wise: it was all quite laid back, with no deadlines to speak of, other than conference submission deadlines. In the weekends we would escape the White Plains boredom by taking trips to the city, walking in Central Park, and all the touristy places. One such Sunday in early September we went to visit the World Trade Center and took the South Tower elevator to the top floor and strolled on the terrace, taking in the view. The next Tuesday morning we woke up as usual, had breakfast, and I got in my car to go to my office in Hawthorne. I was about half way there, when I turned on the radio; instead of the usual classical music, though, all I could hear was a live report from the city, a terrible accident had just happened, the reporters were saying, a commercial plane had hit the North Tower. I continued driving to work. Sixteen minutes later, though, the South Tower was hit by a second plane. At this point, it became clear this wasn’t an accident. I parked my car and went straight to my manager’s office. He was oblivious to all of that. I told him what was happening, and without hesitation he told me to go home to comfort my expecting wife, she was less than two months away from her due date at that point. So I did. When I got home, Grațiela was watching the news on TV, unable to believe her eyes. For the next few hours we called our parents to tell them we’re OK, and then called all our friends from the city to make sure they are safe, offering to host them in our apartment if needed. Thankfully, they were all living far enough from Ground Zero, as the former site of the World Trade Center became known.

Just a few weeks before we had contacted a real estate agent to help us find a home in Westchester County. When she called us afterwards we told her we’re canceling our search for now, we really didn’t know what was going to happen to America. Nothing was the same after that, newspapers were full of hate for all Muslims, huge Wanted pictures of Osama bin Laden on their front pages, airport security became draconic, immigration cases were subjected to heightened scrutiny. Luckily, my H1B visa was already issued, but the green card process ended up taking close to five years.

Slowly things got back to some resemblance of normal, and on November 4th we went to the White Plains Hospital and our daughter was born. The first card on her crib said “Baby Girl Mihaila”, and only later they asked her what we were going to call her. “Maria”, we said, “no middle name”. As Grațiela had developed a slight fever during the lengthy delivery, the doctor took Maria to the ICU as a precaution and stuck a bunch of needles and sensors into her tiny body. They discharged Grațiela, though, so we went home baby-less. Grațiela’s mother had already arrived from Romania, to help with the baby. She was terribly disappointed when she saw us, and so were we, although we were mostly exhausted from the sleepless night spent at the hospital. After a couple of days though we were finally allowed to take Maria home! We were overjoyed, but also terrified, there she was, a tiny human, entirely dependent on us.
