My Parents

My father, Gheorghe Mihăilă, was born on September 7, 1930 in Bucharest in a family of peasants from Transylvania. What was a family of peasants doing in Bucharest, you ask? Well, it turns out that they had come to the capital a few years earlier in order to work and save up enough money so they could buy a small piece of land and build a modest house in their home village. They had both found employment in a school, initially as janitors. The school was established by the Oteteleșanu Foundation in order to educate orphan girls. Through hard work, my grandfather had slowly advanced from simple janitor to some sort of supervisor or administrator position and worked there for a few more years after my dad was born. So it was that my dad first learned to speak like a city boy, without the typical Transylvanian accent, and when they eventually moved back, the other village kids were making fun of his speech.

When the time came for him to go to school, at the beginning of September 1937, the principal sent him back home because he was too little and couldn’t reach the floor when sitting at his desk. Since he hadn’t turned 7 yet, the law was leaving it to the discretion of the principal to enroll him that year if he deemed him adequately developed. My dad was so looking forward to starting school that he couldn’t stop crying on the way home. When his father came back from the fields and saw him crying, he took him by the hand to the principal and asked him to reconsider. The principal told him to write a formal request and send it to the school district, but my grandpa said he would need to dictate it to him as he didn’t know how to write, so why go through all the trouble when he could just enroll him and call it a day. The principal laughed and agreed with him.

My dad quickly became the best student in his class, so much so that the teachers were asking him to help the students in the higher grades with their homework. After the first four years of elementary school, the principal came to my grandpa and urged him to send my dad to the city to continue his education. At the time, only the first four years of school were mandatory, and village kids were expected to stop there and go help their families in the fields. My grandpa listened politely to the principal’s case, but said he couldn’t do that because he didn’t have the money needed for tuition and room and board in the city. The principal insisted, saying that there could be some scholarships that would reduce that burden, and that he would help figure it out, if only my grandpa would agree. After a lot of pleading, he eventually agreed.

And so it was that the next fall he and my dad got in the family oxen-pulled carriage and rode to Sighișoara, to enroll in school there. As he didn’t have city clothes, my dad attended the first few weeks of school dressed in his peasant clothes, becoming an instant bully target for the other students. Seeing this, his godfather gave him his old wedding suit, which was turned inside out by a tailor and refurbished for his frame, slightly larger so that it would last longer. He wore that suit until he outgrew it a few years later. In an effort to reduce the financial burden on his parents, my dad skipped ahead two classes, by studying by himself in the summer vacations and passing the exams in the fall, two years in a row.

After graduating from high school, my dad applied for admission to the University of Bucharest, in the School of Foreign Languages, majoring in Russian. That’s where he met my mom, although she wasn’t the first girl he dated. He would later tell me the story of how this other girl gave him an excuse that she had to study one time instead of going out on a date, but he later saw her coming out of the movies with another guy. That was enough for him to break up with her, no explanations from her mattering anymore. He later asked my mom out and they hit it off immediately.

My mom’s story was even more improbable, as life stories go. To understand why, we need to start with her grandparents.

Her grandfather, Mr. Popescu-Dara, was a relatively wealthy landowner in the Buzău county and quite active in politics. According to the family lore, he switched party affiliations quite frequently: as soon as his party won an election, he would switch to the opposition, he just liked the fight, not the power. One spring morning, while his wife was expecting, he sent word from Buzău to her via a messenger that he had invited his party colleagues to lunch at his ranch. My mom’s grandmother, as she didn’t expect visitors that day, had started to boil the winter linen in a large cauldron on the stovetop. She had to remove that cauldron so she and her maid could cook. In her haste, she lifted that heavy cauldron herself and that caused a miscarriage. Despite numerous visits to clinics she was never able to have children afterwards.

During one of these stays at a clinic, her husband impregnated the maid. He confessed to his wife right away and she agreed, under the circumstances, to raise the resulting baby (my grandmother) as her own, without formally adopting her, and without allowing her husband’s name to appear on the birth certificate either. The reasoning from her husband was that they otherwise wouldn’t have anyone to leave their estate to, since they couldn’t have children of their own. However, they agreed that they would adopt her firstborn child and make that child their sole heir, and not the illegitimate daughter. That firstborn child was my mom!

Indeed, they raised my grandmother as their own daughter, and as soon as she turned sixteen, they married her off to a young schoolteacher from another village who had just been hired to teach mathematics in the village elementary school. That was my grandfather, Tănase Popa.

When the war came, the Russian Army was going to pass through their village on the way to Bucharest. My grandfather, hearing this, hastily got all his daughters dressed in rags and covered their faces with soot to make them as unattractive as possible to the soldiers. My mom was fourteen at the time. Luckily, when the soldiers came, they only asked for Vodka and left.

After the war, the communists took the power, under the protection of the Russian tanks which were still stationed in the country. When my mom applied for university, she was turned back because of her adoptive parents wealth, of which she was the only heir. She promptly went to the public notary and donated her entire estate, with all the land and houses to the State. The poor notary tried with all his might to dissuade her, saying: “Miss Violeta, don’t do this. The late Mr. Popescu-Dara would surely turn in his grave if you go through with this! He worked all his life to build this wealth and you’re giving it all away?” My mom, however, was determined. And this is how she was finally admitted to university, after proving she was penniless now. And that’s where she eventually met my father.
