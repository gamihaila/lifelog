Leaving Google

After we got back from Switzerland, at the end of summer in 2016, our team went out to celebrate our recent launch of the improved question answering system at Cascal, an upscale Spanish cuisine restaurant downtown Mountain View. We got seated at a long table in the back of the restaurant. After we finished, I thought I heard a familiar voice and turned around: it was Alon Halevy, who had recently left Google to take on a CEO role at a new research lab opened in Mountain View by Recruit Holdings, a large Japanese conglomerate. He was having lunch with his new team. Naturally, we exchanged a few words about his new venture, and he said “Let’s keep in touch!”. I knew what that meant. I waited for my Google teammates to leave and I said, discreetly: “Sure, you know where to find me.”

And sure enough, after a few days I got an e-mail from him, asking me if I was available for a chat over lunch. We set a time and place for the following week, and we met. There, Alon told me all about the kind of work they were doing at the new lab, and how the Japanese parent company had given him complete freedom to grow the team over the next few years. “If you’re interested, we’d love to invite you to meet the team.”. So I first went there for an informal chat with some of the senior researchers who told me about the work they were doing pushing the boundaries of natural language understanding and building chatbot prototypes for various application domains (this was years before anybody heard of ChatGPT). I was hooked!

After a few days I wrote to Alon that I’d like to go forward, so his office manager booked a proper interview schedule for the following week. I again solved a bunch of design and coding questions on the whiteboard, and also had some more discussions with the researchers. The role I was interviewing for was called “research engineer” and they were specifically looking for someone that can connect the researchers with the engineers. “That’s what I have been doing for the past 16 years at IBM and Google”, I said.

After a couple more weeks, Alon called me on my cellphone:

“Can you talk?”, he opened.

“Let me step outside”, I said, because I was at work.

“OK, here is the deal: everybody in the team is very excited, we’d love for you to join us. What is your current level at Google?”

“I’m a Level 4 software engineer”

“OK, I know from my own experience that leaving Google is not an easy decision, but if you join, we’ll promote you to Level 5 and raise your compensation by 40%. Interested?

“Thank you, Alon! I’ll give you my answer before the end of the week.”, I said.

A bit of background is warranted here. As you saw in the previous chapters I really liked Google: I had a few successful launches, I was respected, and finally feeling my contributions mattered. However, getting to the next level felt out of reach. I already had several career progression conversations with my manager but no concrete plans. That meant my compensation was hitting a ceiling too. Now, I’ve never been one to chase money above all else, but at the same time we were still renting, four years after we sold our house, and the way the Bay Area real estate market was trending, we were never going to catch up.

So I called Alon and told him I’m in. Now I had the delicate task of announcing my resignation. I grabbed my manager, we went for a walk in the beautiful Google campus, and I explained my reasoning. He asked me if there is anything he could do to make me stay. There wasn’t.

So, with a heavy heart, I left Google, the place where I had matured as a professional software engineer.
