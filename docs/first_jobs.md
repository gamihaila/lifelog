I remember our first day at the Research Institute for Informatics: we
were asked to pick a research group to join. I was at a loss: so far
all my research was purely theoretical, using algebraic objects to
describe the structure of flowcharts. Nobody was working on anything
remotely related to that in the institute, they were mostly focused on
more traditional areas like Operating Systems, Compilers, Networks,
and Information Systems. That's when Dana said:

"George, how about Databases? Every application needs to persist state
somehow, you'll always be in demand. It's not Abstract Algebra, but
it's useful. And I heard that it has a theoretical side to it
too. That's what I'm picking."

At that point, I knew next to nothing about databases, only that I
tried and failed to convert the CS library catalog into a searchable
database, as a school project. I had never seen a commercial database
system ever. I heard of a thing called Oracle, but I don't think it
was installed on our school mainframe, and even if it was, us students
didn't have access to it. However, Dana's arguments resonated with me,
after spending the past couple of years wondering whether my work
could have any possible applications, I was attracted by the idea of
working in a more applied area. So we went together to talk to the
manager of the Database Research group. I don't know what kind of vibe
we were giving, because, after he listened to us he said:

"It's great that you're interested in this area, there are a lot of
interesting problems to solve. I do have one thing I want to warn the
two of you about, though: are you sure you want to work in the same
team and see each other all the time at work too?"

It took us a second to process his question, and then we answered in
unison:

"Oh, we're not married!"

"Oh, sorry", said the manager, slightly confused, "in that case,
welcome to our team!"

So we joined the Database Research group and got assigned seats in
their lab right away.  There was however no on-boarding, no
mentorship, and no oversight to our work. I think I did less work
there than ever before and nobody was really interested. It was quite
a letdown after all the hard classes and the undergraduate research I
did. I kept busy implementing side projects for fun on the work PC,
and getting used to the newest peripheral, the mouse, of course in
text mode, but still a great improvement over arrow keys. The job was
thoroughly unispiring and I started looking for other opportunities
that could be more exciting.

One of my former professors, I forgot which one, told me that he
received an inquiry from the Romanian Television about an opening
there for a Tele-school program teaching Math and he recommended me
for it. I was familiar with this educational program since childhood:
it had been running continuously, and was covering the standard middle
and high school curriculum, at a high standard. I haven't been
watching it regularly myself, but every time I tuned in by chance and
happened to see it, I was impressed by the quality of the teaching. So
I went there for a preliminary interview, more out of curiosity than
thinking I actually wanted to get that job. At the time, this was the
only TV station in the country, a beloved institution that had kept my
generation glued to the screen watching amazing science shows like
Carl Sagan's Cosmos and James Burke's Connections, in addition to the
Romanian Tele-encyclopedia and the excellent TV plays and comedy shows
that helped keep our sanity during the darkest Communist years. So,
once I passed the security checkpoint, I walked into the Television
main tower and took the elevator to my interview room. I remember
sharing that elevator ride with a couple of famous comedy actors,
awestruck by their unassuming demeanor. I finally got into the
interview room: siting accross from me, behind a huge desk, there was
the Tele-school program director, who started laying our the job
benefits (some paltry salary bonus for television workers), as well as
the specifics of the job: making sure to plan your lesson content well
in advance, book the studio on time (competing with the other shows),
record the lesson, tape wathever re-takes were needed, and make sure
to submit the final tape at least a week before airing time. I felt
an intense stress just listening to him talk. We talked a bit about my
credentials and experince, then I thanked him for his time, and told
him this was not for me. I walked out, seeing some more actors in
the hallway, but never looked back: as fun as it may be to rub elbows
with celebrities, it wasn't worth the constant stress.

At the same time an opening became available for a permanent teaching
position at the university, at the entry level called preparatory
teaching assistant, but on the career ladder for assistant professor,
lecturer, and eventually full professor. Naturally, I submitted my
application for that position, it was not only what I had been raised
to do since childhood, but I also had these early research
publications that were encouraging me in this direction, as well as
top grades in my degree. The contest for this position included a
written exam covering the entire four-year Computer Science
curriculum. So I set to work reviewing all my lecture notes and the
textbooks while I was at my day job at the Institute, thinking nothing
of it, since I didn't have any other job responsibilities anyways. I
was studying Computer Science, wasn't I? Wasn't this what the Institue
was devoted to? Right. So, I did go to take that written exam and got
the job. For a few more weeks I still kept my job at the Insitute, but
I eventually left, not because I couldn't have both jobs at the same
time, but out of intense boredom.  Dana left around the same time too:
she had applied for graduate school in France, and had obtained a
scholarship at the famous École Normale Supérieure in Paris.

At the university I was assigned as an assistant to prof. Căzănescu,
teaching the recitation sessions. It was a lot of fun, helping the
upper division students decipher the same Abstract Algebra and Category
Theory topics I had been plowing through when working with him in his
small apartment the years before.


At the time I was still enrolled in the fifth year at the university,
a sort of masters program. I remember we were in the final week before
graduation when a young woman came into our classroom during a break
and said something like this, with no introductions:

"Hello, is this the Informatics Fifth Year class?"

"Yes, it is. Who are you?", I remember asking.

"My name is Ana, and I came here to recruit software engineers
for my brother's start-up. Who are the top three graduating students
in your cohort?"

"Petrișor, Cristi and myself", I said, pointing to my classmates.

"Well, my brother has founded a start-up last year and at the moment
we have more computers than people. If you're interested, we would
love to have you visit our lab. Here is our address and phone
number. When would it be a good time?"

"We have our final exams next week but we can come the week after", we
all aggreed.

So, on the appointed day, the three of us went together to the address
on the card and met with her brother, the founder, and the handful of
engineers working there. The company was called Omnis Group and they
were working with some companies and universities in Belgium,
developing some applications for them. The office was in a converted
four bedroom apartment in a residential high-rise building, and indeed
they had about ten Windows PCs, a print server, a couple of printers,
a modem, and a local area Ethernet network connecting
everything. Aside from the founder, there were about five engineers
working there. They also had an impressive collection of American
software engineering books and magazines, which was unheard of in
1991. After an informal chat with the founder we were all hired on a
trial basis, with a basic official salary in lei which was already
more than my pay as a TA at the university, supplemented by a monthly
pay in dollars, completely off the books. Moreover, the dollar amount
was going to be increasing every month for the first year, provided
that we had a satisfactory performance.

This was not that unusual in those early years of free market
capitalism after ther revolution, where the many start-up firms that
had sprung up were competing for the relatively few engineers on the
market. Of course, it was all very hush-hush, we were just paid in
cash with no paper trail, and we were not supposed to talk about
it. At first, I thought that was quite sketchy and I was afraid that
the shadow payments will dry out eventually, but they kept coming,
month after month, each month more than the previous one. They weren't
large amounts, in absolute terms, on the order of a hundred dollars a
month, but they were more dollars that I had ever seen in my life, and
the exchange rate was insanely favorable, what with the national
currency in free fall and all. We were living it up, very soon
I was making more money than my parents combined.

At Omnis Group is where I first saw a graphical operating system, the
new Microsoft Windows 3.1, in 1991. I learned how to write programs
for Windows from the Charles Petzold book, Programming Windows. At the
time, the programs were single threaded, and the book taught us to
write event handlers that returned control quickly because otherwise
the operating system would be frozen. These were interactive programs:
a user would push a button on the UI, or select a menu item, something
needed to happen in response, and then the program would wait for the
next user interaction. So I used this paradigm to implement some sort
of accounting application for a Belgian oil refinery that was backed
by a network database called dbVista.

There were tools for designing the GUI visually and converting it to
runnable assets accessible from C code. I also started learning C++
there, from Bjarne Stroustrup’s book. I needed that for the next
project, a research GUI for analyzing proteins. There were some
edge-detection algorithms there, but I don’t remember much about that
project other than the fact we were using graphical workstations
running X11 and the mice were optical with a special mouse pad with a
grid on it. I also had my first contact there with running remote
sessions over the LAN on my colleagues' workstations. We were playing
pranks on each other by opening windows on someone else’s screen. It
was there I first heard of telnet and ftp. No email just yet, since
our LAN was not permanently connected to any external network. There
was an occasional connection through a modem and a leased telephone
line, I heard, but that wasn’t part of my day-to-day work.

I was working two jobs at this point: full time software engineer at
Omins Group and part-time teaching assistant at the University. I was
also supposedly continuing my collaboration with prof. Căzănescu, with
the understanding that I would eventually work towards a doctoral
dissertation under his supervision. In fact, he once bumped into my
dad in the corridor connecting the Math wing with the Letters wing in
the Edgar Quinet university building and asked him directly what my
plans were, implying that I wasn't making enough progress. Needless to
say, this upset me to no end, I didn't need that kind of pressure on
top of what I was going through on my own.

This wasn't sustainable, and after a year of running around between my
two jobs and having little to no time left for research I started
thinking of leaving my job at Omnis Group so I could focus on my
academic career. With my limited knowledge of the Bible, this verse
came to mind: "No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the
one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise
the other." I am aware that this was meant to refer to choosing
between God and money, but at the time I felt it applied quite
literally to my situation. As I was aspiring to an academic career, I
slowly started to despise my job at Omnis, especially when I had
stopped learning new things and it felt I was just coding, with no
feedback from senior engineers and no visibility into the final
utility of my efforts.

At some point, the office moved to a larger apartment as the team
grew. Some of my former colleagues from the Informatics Research
Institute were poached too, including a girl from my former database
research group. Her name was Elena, but everyone called her by the
diminutive Lenuș. She was assigned to pair program with me in C++, and
that made the job more enjoyable. At some point, out Belgian
colleagues visited and delivered a code review after four months of
our development. I remember not being happy with thir critique of our
design, but thinking back now, they were probably right, it's just
that we would have benefited from getting it earlier so we could align
to the expected style.

At some point there was some deep cleaning of the office and all
windows were left wide open to dry the floor. All good, except that it
was early spring and I was sitting directly next to the window. The
next day I had a high fever and persistent cough. It was much
worse than any cold I had before, so I went to see a doctor (Ioana's
dad drove me to the clinic and waited for me). Turns out I had
pneumonia, so I got admitted to the hospital. I was placed in a room
with another pneumonia patient, an older man who couldn't stop
caughing and wheezing, in much worse condition than me. After a couple
of days I wasn't getting any better, the antibiotics I was treated
with intravenously not doing anything except giving me an intense
itching. Ioana visited me once but complained it was too inconvenient
to get to the hospital by bus. In my feverish state, this didn't
exactly raise my spirits. Finally, my sister talked to a family
friend, a doctor who contacted my doctor and suggested a stronger
antibiotic. It was only when my sister brought an envelope with bribe money
for my doctor that she finally switched my treatment and, surprise, I
started getting better immediately. I was released after one full
week, left the hospital on my own, and went home (Ioana was at
work, teaching).

Not too long after that, Ioana asked me to help her with a programming
project, and I ran her program on my work computer. My boss, who was
already aware that I was no longer too enthusiastic about my job, used
this incident as an excuse to fire me on the spot, and make an example
to the other engineers that nobody is allowed to work on any personal
coding project on company time. When he asked me theatrically to lift
my hands off the keyboard, I looked at him, with gratitude in my eyes
and said:

"Thank you! I wanted to leave this job for a while now, and you just
saved me the trouble of bringing this up."

He stared at me in disbelief, but I totally meant it, I was happy to
leave, thinking that now I would finally have enough time for my
research work.

I was wrong!  My in-laws started to ask me to run errands for them the
moment I was out from my full-time job: I apparently had too much time
on my hands. Also, the savings from my high paying gig eventually
drained up and we could no longer afford the nice life we had just
started getting used to, on Ioana's teacher salary and my TA stipend.
I remember clearly one time I was walking with Ioana on our way to
home, and we passed by a nice pastry shop with tables outside and we
wanted to stop by and have a treat. At the last moment, though, I
realized I didn't have enough cash with me, and also probably not
enough at home either. This was way before credit cards became
available in Romania and also before ATMs, so no cash meant no
pastries for us. It was thoroughly embarassing for me, I'd never been
penniless since high school, especially after starting working.  Come
to think of it, back then, around 1992, we were still living in a
cash-only economy in Romania. Hard to imagine, I know, but we didn't
experience anything else our entire lives, so it didn't seem strange
to us. The first time we became aware that there were such things as
credit cards was when we watched a pirated VHS copy of Trading Places
at a friend's place! And even there, the impression we got was that
these things could be revoked and your accounts frozen if your
employer wanted to mess with you, for no reason at all other than
turning you in a guinea pig for a silly bet dressed as "behavioural
science experiment". So we walked away from that movie with a deep
distrust in banks. Now, this is not to say that nobody had any
deposits, we did, but the only institution that was available was the
state-owned Savings House, which offered an interest rate so low that
one would effectively lose money to inflation on any longterm
deposit. So everyone was hoarding cash instead.

I clearly needed another full-time job, and fast. Hearing me say that,
our old friend Claudia offered to talk to her boss about me. She was a
salesperson at the Romanian branch of Ciel!, a French accounting
software company. And wouldn't you know it, he called me for an
interview the very next week! On the appointed day I went to their
office in the Bucharest Hotel, remembering how I had carried bricks
when the hotel was being built during my high school mandatory
"volunteering" weeks (we were all taken out of regular classes for two
weeks to work on various construction sites each year).

Anyway, I got to the manager's office and he showed me the company
software suite on his PC. It was a comprehensive set of accounting
applications they were adapting for the Romanian small businesses, all
running in text mode, with drop down menus and text-based input
forms. I looked at him and said: "This is good, but nowadays the
competition has modern GUIs running inside Microsoft Windows. Give me
six months and I will rewrite one of these applications completely
from scratch in Windows, as a proof of concept." He hired me on the
spot.

There I worked on porting to Windows an old text-mode Clipper
accounting application called “Ciel! Dossiers Analytiques”. One
thing I remember about that was the trouble caused by rounding for
accounting applications, where the monetary amounts had to match
precisely, down to cents.

I remember there was this constant friendly teasing between the
software people and the sales people about who's contribution was more
important: of course we were arguing that without us, there would be
nothing to sell, but Claudia was pointing out that our software would
be worthless if she and her peers didn't get the customers to pay for
the license. She had a point!
