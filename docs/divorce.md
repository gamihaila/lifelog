It was the spring of 1996. One sunny weekend day, as I was strolling
with my wife in Lakeshore Park in Toronto, I asked her, for the
millionth time, if there was anything going on between her and our
friend Jan.

“What if there was?” was her answer.

“Oh, that’s new,” I thought to myself, “she always denied before.” “So
it is true, after all?” I asked.

“Yes,” she finally admitted.

“Okay, so now what? Where do we go from here?”

“I don’t quite know,” she said, “I still have feelings for you.”

“Well, then, can’t you break up with him and we try to fix our
marriage?”

“Unfortunately, no, I don’t think I can,” she replied.

“Come on, whose girl are you?”, I tried to lighten up the mood,
reminding her how she always answered “Yours!” for the past ten years.

“I’m mine!”, Ioana replied this time. Not someone else’s, her own
girl, standing on her own two feet. I was struck by her answer, but
couldn’t help appreciate its truth. She was her own person, of course.
Nobody owns anyone. She used to be mine by choice, all these years,
and now she wasn’t. As simple as that! I couldn’t even be angry at
her.

Thinking about it now, I realize she was right, I never truly loved
her, I was simply used to her and too scared of being alone. All these
years I didn’t give her what she needed and that was what created the
cracks in our union, which slowly deepened right before my eyes. Her
affair was never the cause of our troubles, it was its consequence.

Only later did I realize that I was well into the classic “five stages
of grief.” Having finished the denial and anger stages, I was entering
the bargaining stage. I actually pleaded with her to stay with me:
“Take some time to reflect on this, please.” I took the Greyhound bus
to Hamilton to stay with her sister and her husband for a few days, in
order to give her some space. It didn’t help.

When I came back, she was already packed and ready to move out. We sat
down on the couch in our living room and hugged for a long time. Then
she left for good.

I remember I had to pick up something from some Romanian family the
next day, and I was in complete shock. Life was continuing its course
but I felt completely disoriented. Although I had been expecting this
for a long time already, when it finally happened it felt as if I had
been hit by a truck. I was trying really hard not to cry; I was in
some stranger’s house, they didn’t know anything. When I finally got
home I got into bed and sobbed, crying myself to sleep.

I awake the next day with a strange feeling: I was on my own, for the
first time in years. I went for a long walk downtown, and as I was
wandering aimlessly I saw a poster for a David Copperfield magic show.
That's when it hit me: I can just buy a ticket and go, on an impulse,
without asking anybody! So I went. The show exceeded all my
expectations: I had seen David Copperfield on TV many times, but
seeing his magic acts up close and personal was a completely different
feeling! Once it was over, I was feeling a lot better and I started
looking for more shows to go to.

After a few more days, as I was adjusting to my new status, I tried
really hard to see the bright side of this: now that it had happened
it was no longer a looming threat, and it felt easier to bear,
somehow. I started thinking of the positives: I was no longer required
to tell anybody what time I’d be home, I could go and do whatever I
wanted, I could meet whomever I wanted, make new friends and even date
again.

That prospect was a bit scary though: aside from Ioana I had never
really dated anyone before, and I was now 29 years old. I had not had
a new girlfriend for the past ten years, in fact. Add to that the
small detail that I was in a foreign country, still relatively a
newcomer, with basically no friends and no family. And what did I know
about the dating protocol in Canada anyway?

I started spending a lot of time in bookstores, especially the shiny
new Chapters bookstore on Bloor Street, which had comfortable
armchairs one could sit in for hours. While browsing aimlessly I found
the Self-Help section and one title caught my attention: "Divorce for
Beginners: How to Get Unhitched Without the Hitches", by Cathy
Hopkins. It was a cartoon book that used humor to discuss the
practical aspects of getting it together after a divorce. It was just
what I needed! Humor is what I always fell back on, and it has never
failed me.

Now that I was feeling a little better I thought to contact my former
University of Bucharest schoolmate Dana, who was doing a PhD in Paris.
So I wrote her an email to let her know Ioana had left me and asked
her if we could talk. In 1996 international phone calls were expensive
and there was no WhatsApp. But there was a live chat terminal-based
Unix program called “talk,” and it was free. So Dana wrote back to me
and we set up a time when we could both log in and have a live text
session. This was an exciting new development at the time, being able
to text in real time for free over the Atlantic!

So, at the appointed time we started our “talk” session. After a few
pleasantries, I went straight into the heart of the matter:

“Dana, did you know that I had a big crush on you all through
college?”

“Yes, I know,” she said, “it was hard not to notice, but what did you
expect me to do about it? You were always with Ioana.”

She was right. Even though I was very attracted to her I never dared
to tell her. She seemed completely out of reach for me. She had a
boyfriend too. Also, I liked her so much I was never able to have a
relaxed conversation with her; I just froze in front of her.

What happened later took me by surprise, to say the least. After we
exhausted the subject of my infatuation with her, Dana said out of the
blue:

“Why don’t you come over to Paris for an internship at INRIA? It would
take your mind off of things.”

“How would I do that?” I asked. “I don’t know anyone there.”

“No, but they know you.”

“Huh, you’re kidding, right?”

“No, I’m not. It so happens that your PhD advisor came last month and
gave a talk about your research at INRIA. I was there in the room, and
my advisor asked a lot of questions. I can let him know that you are
available for a possible internship, if you want.”

“Wow, that would be fantastic, thanks Dana!” I said.

A few weeks later I got an official invitation letter from Patrick
Valduriez, and I started booking my flights and accommodation in
Paris. I decided to use this opportunity to also visit my parents in
Bucharest before Paris.

So in the course of a few weeks after Ioana left, I received the
official divorce papers (no-fault divorce with no communal property to
divide), signed them, and was getting ready for my trip to Europe!

It happened that Ioana was also going on vacation to Bucharest at the
same time and we figured out that while we're both there we should set
things straight with the Romanian authorities too. You see, even
though we were already divorced in Canada, it turns out that in
Romania we were still legally married. I suppose we could have applied
to get our Canadian divorce papers translated and notarized in
Romania, but that was a lengthly process. The easiest way, by far, was
to file for divorce with the Bucharest Family Court, especially since
our marriage was registered there. So we went in front of a judge for
a preliminary hearing. I remember we were very civil to each other, to
the point that the judge was genuinely puzzled why we were there: "Are
you really sure you want to do this? You seem to be on such good terms
with each other. Please take some time and maybe you can reconsider."
We didn't want to complicate matters so we didn't mention that we were
already divorced in Canada, and left her office after she set the date
for a follow-up hearing. As this date was after we were due back in
Toronto, I gave power of attorney to my sister, and Ioana to her dad.
So six months later, my sister and Ioana's dad got us divorced by
proxy.
