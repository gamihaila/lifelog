Now that I had passed the university admission exam, you'd think I
would go to college right away, but that's not how things worked at
the time. You see, after high school, all boys were required to enroll
in full-time military training first. The only difference the
university admission made was that instead of going for a one and a
half years of training, as future students we were assigned to a
"reduced term" unit, that was only nine months long. We were assigned
to specific units according to our major: for Mathematics, that meant
an Artillery unit.

So after spending the summer with my family (we went on trip to Sofia,
Bulgaria, following my dad who was sent there for research by the
university), I had to pack and get ready to join the Army. That meant
a wood suitcase, with a lock, packed with essentials, but not clothes.
My father went with me by train to Focșani, a small town three hours
North-East of home, where I was scheduled to report for duty.

Once at the unit, I said goodbye to him, and joined my fellow
recruits, who were all Math and CS students admitted that year at the
University of Bucharest. Some of them I already recognized from the
free admission prep classes I had attended in spring: one of them I
knew well, he attended all the sessions with me, his name was Radu,
and we became good friends (I still keep in touch with him to this
day, he lives in New York).

We were all getting buzz haircuts and new uniforms, including
underwear, socks, boots and caps. On the caps we were all fighting to
get the largest ones to cover our newly bald heads. People would steal
each others caps, whenever we were indoors and left the caps on the
tables. So we quickly started signing our cap inside with markers, but
not before I managed to swap my cap for a larger one from an
unsuspecting recruit. Sorry, but extreme situations call for extreme
solutions.

After a couple of weeks of basic instruction, we were all loaded into
busses and taken to a village, to help with the harvest. We spent four
weeks there, in an apple orchard, picking apples all day. The work
itself was not that hard, we had bags attached to our necks and we
would climb ladders and pick a bag full of apples, then get down and
unload our bag into a container, and climb back up. The officers were
keeping tallies and each evening a leaderboard would get updated with
the top pickers. I didn't care for getting on the top of that
leaderboard, so I picked at a leisurely pace, as did most of my peers,
with few exceptions that were consistently on top (I'll never know
what motivated these kids, but good for them, I guess). I never ate
this many apples in my life, they were really delicious, a real treat,
especially after the army food we were served. After we got back to
the barracks in Focșani I realized that food was actually more
nutritious than what we got afterwards, during regular training: each
meal, breakfast, lunch and dinner we had a variety of bean-based
recipes, nothing else, except for the occasional fish cans and bread.

So after the harvest session, we were back in the barracks for the
actual instruction: half of it was in-class and the other half was
outdoors. Oh, how we treasured those in-class hours, especially in the
winter! It didn't matter what the subject was, even political
indoctrination courses were preferable to freezing in the fields. Most
of the classes were teaching us how to calculate and adjust the
trajectories of cannon projectiles, taking into account the wind speed
as reported by the military weather reports, and using detailed
topographic maps and coordinates. As we couldn't rely on calculators
on the battlefield, we were trained to do all the calculations by
hand, using logarithm tables to speed up multiplications (by adding
the corresponding logarithms and then looking up the result in the
inverse logarithm table). With a lot of practice, we got quite fast at
it too. That's why they had picked Math majors for Artillery, now it
made sense. That part was actually fun, learning how to use the
topographic instruments in the field to triangulate our position using
known landmarks, and then calculate the exact angles for the cannon to
reach a target with known coordinates, this was the only time I
actually got first-hand experience applying geometry in real life! I
could not have imagined at the time that forty years later I would be
using the same geo-coordinates from GPS-enabled iPhones for my job at
Apple Maps.

Alas, it was not all just theoretical training in the Army! We spent
an inordinate amount of time outside, cleaning the huge cannons,
endlessly perfecting the military salute with the Kalashnikov rifle,
marching, running, wearing the gas masks, and occasionally firing our
rifles and hand pistols at a target. But all that wasn't even the
hardest part, as we would soon find out.

What could be worse, you ask? Two words: sentinel service! In the
minus thirty degrees weather, up in the sentinel tower, in the middle
of the night. The shift from 2 to 5 am was the worst of all. We had
three hour shifts, followed by six hour rest, for three days in a row.
After each of these, I was looking forward to the regular instruction,
even the outdoors one. It turns out that Einstein was right:
everything was relative. No matter how bad a situation is, we were
learning that there can always be a worse one. Case in point: I was
certain that nothing can be worse than the night shift in the cold
winter. One time though, I was assigned to guard the flag, indoors,
from 2 to 5 am. At first, I couldn't believe my luck: I was indoors,
while my peers were in the cold windy towers. After half an hour of
standing "at attention" in that warm room, with the commander on duty
patrolling back and forth at irregular intervals, my eyes could not
stay open, I had an overpowering sleepiness but I had to stay awake
and alert. It was torture. I would trade that for the tower anytime.

After a few months of theoretical training, we went into the mountains
and actually fired some canons at targets, using our new skills. We
failed miserably, all our projectiles fell far away from the targets,
to the great embarrassment of our commanding officers in front of
their superiors.

Finally, the nine months were coming to an end, and we could hardly
contain our excitement. I remember one of our commanding officers
getting out of character and uttering this remark, which could have
gotten him arrested, if anyone reported him to the Securitate: "Kids,
you are so naive, you think you're escaping this prison, but what you
don't realize is that you're just moving into a much larger prison."
This was in 1986, three years before the fall of Communism.
